Good afternoon, everyone!

I am Father Francesco De Vita, a priest of the Diocese of San Severo since 1987 and a member of the Cenacolisti Missionary Movement. I studied at the Pontifical Roman Major Seminary in Rome. I obtained a licentiate in Theology of Marriage and the Family and a Master's degree in Bioethics. I currently serve as parish priest at the parish of Maria Santissima della Fontana in Torremaggiore. I also work as an ethical adviser at the diocesan counselling centre, and I am responsible for the continuing formation of the clergy and young priests, as well as the diocesan delegate for religious life. For many years I was in charge of the family ministry office and the diocesan family counselling centre.

I spent seven years in Benin as a fidei donum priest and, when I returned, I worked in vocations ministry.

During my years in family ministry, my path crossed that of Retrouvaille, but the real encounter came later, when I was responsible for vocations ministry. At that time, I attended a meeting introducing the Retrouvaille programme at the Pontifical Major Seminary in Molfetta. There I came to understand deeply the value of this journey for couples in crisis. Shortly afterwards, a couple telephoned to ask whether I would be willing to serve in Retrouvaille. I attended the basic weekend. When I returned, I made myself available to serve families within Retrouvaille, standing alongside wounded couples so that they would not feel alone or abandoned.

Recently, I accepted the call to serve on the coordinating team for the Southern Italy community. After seeking light in prayer, I said my “yes”, aware that I am like a child learning to take his first steps, entrusting myself entirely to the Holy Spirit, the light and guide of my journey.

2. TALK BY FATHER FRANCESCO DE VITA

I am happy to spend these days of the meeting with you, and I feel moved by this moment of sharing. Thank you for the trust you have placed in me.

There is a biblical image dwelling in my heart as I stand here with you in this room: the image of the man who could not walk at the Beautiful Gate of Jerusalem, begging for help in his weakness (cf. Acts 3:6). The apostle Peter addressed words to him that I feel called to make my own today. Paraphrasing his words, I say to each of you: “I have neither silver nor gold, but what I have I give you: I offer you what I have discovered by walking alongside the many couples I have met in my ministry: the beauty of marriage and the family.”

This beauty is not a utopia, but a living reality made of patience, forgiveness, and glances that meet and recognize one another even in the darkness. It is the beauty of two stories choosing to become one, like two tree trunks whose branches intertwine into a single canopy; of a love that takes flesh in children, that becomes a refuge in storms and a celebration on sunny days.

We are here to continue walking with hearts full of hope.

We have lived through the Jubilee of Hope. We have passed through the Door. The journey we have made cannot remain in a photo album of memories; it must become a fruitful seed in the everyday life of our marriages. This is the time for work after sowing. The seed that falls into the soil contains within itself the promise of fruit. We are called to cultivate the soil of our marital relationship because that is where the seed has fallen. It is beautiful to cultivate that soil with a sense of transition, because every passage in life or faith is always an opportunity for growth. Married life is not a finishing line but a journey of growth on which spouses are called to walk together, day after day.

In John's Gospel, Jesus says: “Very truly, I tell you, unless a grain of wheat falls into the earth and dies, it remains alone; but if it dies, it bears much fruit” (John 12:24). Only by dying can the seed give life to the ear of wheat and its fruit. Cultivating the soil so that the seed bears fruit means allowing God to accomplish the work he has begun in us, so that we may become a credible sign of his love, since married life is a manifestation of God's own love and an instrument of sanctification for spouses.

Taking care of the soil in which the seed has fallen becomes the essential part of our work, transforming the grace we have received into a new way of life. This is a task that the couple does not undertake alone, but with the support of Hope, who is Christ.

Saint Paul reminds us in his Letter to the Romans: “Hope does not disappoint us, because God's love has been poured into our hearts through the Holy Spirit that has been given to us” (Romans 5:5).

The apostle Paul invites us to give a reason for the hope that is within us. In the marital relationship, this means recognizing the presence of God, who is faithful and always at work. This presence is the anchor that allows a couple to look beyond their weaknesses.

It is precisely by holding on to this certainty that the journey of healing produces concrete fruit, becoming day by day an entirely new way of living the relationship.

When Hope inhabits the reality of daily life, it blossoms into real choices of renewal. This continuing journey allows couples to shape a renewed way of life, where a wound becomes an opening through which light can enter.

In Retrouvaille we have learned and experienced that healing a relationship is not the result of waving a magic wand; rather, it is the fruit of genuine craftsmanship, a commitment that cannot be occasional but must be chosen and renewed every single day.

We are called to embody a new way of life that tells of the beauty of marriage as an intimate communion of life and love, where our home becomes, day after day, the face of a small domestic church.

Together with you, I would like to outline this new way of life against the background of the everyday life of the first Christian community in Jerusalem, as described in the Acts of the Apostles (2:42–48).

Let us listen to the biblical passage.

They devoted themselves to the apostles' teaching and fellowship, to the breaking of bread and the prayers. Awe came upon everyone, and many wonders and signs were done by the apostles. All who believed were together and had all things in common; they sold their possessions and goods and distributed the proceeds to all, as any had need. Day by day, as they spent much time together in the temple, they broke bread at home and ate their food with glad and generous hearts, praising God and having the goodwill of all the people. And day by day the Lord added to their number those who were being saved (Acts 2:42–48).

This text is known as the “first summary” of the early Christian community. It offers us a broad view of the characteristics of life in the first community of Jerusalem.

Perseverance, communion, sharing, the breaking of bread and prayer characterized the everyday life of the first Christian community. Every member of the community played a part in giving shape to those characteristics.

Everything depends on us.

The first believers remained together not because of family ties, affection, social or political reasons, or friendship, but because they shared their faith and daily life, and persisted in their decision “to remain together, to be of one heart and one soul.” Perseverance is essential in the commitment to build a new way of married life, going beyond merely living under the same roof and opening ourselves to a communion of hearts and intentions. In Familiaris Consortio, John Paul II reminds us: “The first communion is the one established and developed between husband and wife: by virtue of the covenant of married love, the man and woman are no longer two but one flesh (Matt 19:6; cf. Gen 2:24), and they are called to grow continually in their communion through day-to-day fidelity to their marriage promise of total mutual self-giving” (FC 19). This commitment is not for one, two or three days, but forever.

In our biblical passage, perseverance is expressed through verbs such as “were”, “happened”, “stayed”, “attended” and “broke bread”, emphasizing the continuity of how they lived. Luke, the author of Acts, used these verbs to tell us that this community, in the way it lives, should be welcomed as a model for the everyday life of marriage. It is as if Luke had handed us a mirror in which to look at ourselves. Such a community is possible.

In everyday married life, perseverance and fidelity are the roots of communion: “I take you as my spouse and promise to be faithful to you always, not for just one day, not only when you satisfy me in everything, but even when you say no to me, and even when pain knocks on the door of our home.” It is an invitation to persevere in the decision to love, because this love is a gift of the grace of the sacrament of marriage, which enables spouses to live a new life.

We read in Acts: “They devoted themselves to the apostles' teaching and fellowship, to the breaking of bread and the prayers.”

This verse tells us that perseverance in listening to the apostles' teaching guided the Christians' practical choices and encouraged care for the bonds among the members of the community.

Listening to the Word together leads us beyond individualism. There is no longer room for my project, my expectations, my needs or my finances; instead, we must live our project for life, our expectations, our needs and our shared finances, because everything must be welcomed and kept within an “ours”. It means sharing our experiences, weaknesses and joys.

When sharing is authentic, a husband will never come home and say to his wife, “I have bought a new car,” without first having discussed with her whether or not that was the right thing to do.

Sharing leads us to break bread together. Breaking bread was the most important thing the early Christian communities possessed. It was the visible sign of their communion. They were communities that set an example of true fraternity, drawing the attention of those who observed them. For a married couple, breaking bread takes shape in the spouses' ability to “break” themselves for one another, freely and joyfully, participating in the love with which “Christ loved his Bride and gave himself for her” (LG 41).

Spouses who attend the Eucharist together, just as the first community went to the temple together, experience being united as the body of Christ, feeling themselves to be “of one heart and one soul.”

By taking part in the Eucharist together, spouses find the true nourishment that helps them grow in giving themselves to each other forever, just as Christ in the Eucharist becomes body given and blood poured out for us, giving himself to us. Loving and being loved express the reciprocity of self-giving, because the other is “bone of my bones and flesh of my flesh” (Gen 2:23). Through the Eucharist, spouses gain the ability to live with joyful dedication each day, experiencing mutual belonging in love and the communion of hearts. To love one another as spouses means to meet, give, welcome and care for one another with gladness and simplicity of heart. Pope Leo XIV writes: “When husband and wife give themselves totally and mutually in love, God becomes present in their midst. Their fidelity, their forgiveness, their daily struggle for good are an image of Christ's love for his Church. This may seem like romantic idealization, but it expresses a profound truth about reality: ‘In the one Christ we are one’” (Saint Augustine, Exposition on Psalm 127, 3; preface to Youcat, Love forever).

The first Christians prayed together. For a couple, praying together is not simply a question of praying alongside the other person, but praying with them, bringing the struggles and joys of married life before God. In Amoris Laetitia, Pope Francis writes that “family concerns should not be something foreign to their style of spiritual life. The Lord's presence dwells in real and concrete families, with all their sufferings, struggles, joys and daily hopes” (nos. 313 and 315).

The closing verse of our text says: “Having the goodwill of all the people. And day by day the Lord added to their number those who were being saved.”

The way of life of the first Christian community becomes a proclamation of the Gospel, a path of evangelization. Thanks to their witness, the number of believers grew day by day. The first community was not a closed circle. Its life touched those who came near.

A couple who looks to the first Christian community in Jerusalem becomes good news of God's merciful love, which calls us to perseverance, communion and sharing. Such a couple can inspire the men and women of our time to fall in love with the beauty of marriage as a school of perseverance, communion and sharing.

Your marital relationship, which is a sacrament, can show the world that marriage is not a limitation but a wonderful school. A school where:

We learn perseverance, keeping our promise in moments of joy and of hardship.

We cultivate communion, discovering ourselves deeply united in our thoughts, expectations, projects, desires and hearts.

We practise sharing, putting our life in common, with its struggles and hopes.

Be this beauty. Be this good news that touches those around you.

I would like to conclude this sharing by recalling an extraordinary anniversary: this year marks eight hundred years since Saint Francis received the stigmata at La Verna. That seal of love on the saint's body inevitably takes us back to the beginnings of his vocation, and so I would like to recall the image of the Crucifix of San Damiano.

Let us imagine, right now, stopping and looking at that Crucifix. Let us look in particular at the eyes of Jesus.

They are not eyes closed in death, but open and alive. They gaze between heaven and earth, toward the future, toward fulfilment. His gaze calls eternity to mind. On the cross, Jesus looks beyond death; he already sees the resurrection and the fullness of life that springs from the gift of self.

For each of you, for your married life, all this has immense meaning: it means directing your gaze between the present and the future, toward the fulfilment of the beauty of marriage and family.

I wish that you may make the gaze of the Crucifix of San Damiano your own and always preserve the ability to look beyond, walking together, knowing that your story is moving toward a greater beauty, toward a fullness built day by day, with eternity as its horizon.

May you continue well on your journey, and thank you for your attention.
